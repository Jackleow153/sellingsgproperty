Thomson Reserve Price: Is It Really Too Expensive in 2026?

Let's start with the honest version. Yes, Thomson Reserve will likely launch in the S$2,500 to S$3,100 psf range. Yes, that feels heavy. And yes, if you have been watching the Upper Thomson corridor for years, the new pricing can give you that quiet, uneasy "am I being asked to pay tomorrow's price today?" feeling.

That feeling is rational. Hold onto it for a moment. We will look at it from every angle in this post.

But here is what often gets missed. "Expensive" and "overpriced" are not the same word. A project can be expensive and still be the right buy — if the entry point unlocks something the cheaper alternatives cannot. A project can also be cheap and still be the wrong buy — if your money sits still for ten years because nothing structural is changing around it.

What follows is a fair-minded look at every worry a careful Singaporean buyer is right to raise about Thomson Reserve, and what the numbers actually say once you push past the headlines. By the end, you should know whether this launch is for you — or whether the next one is.

Figures referenced come from publicly available URA, EdgeProp, 99.co, IRAS, and developer-side data as of mid-2026. Launch pricing is indicative until the official release.

What Thomson Reserve Actually Is (Before We Debate the Price)

Quick orientation, in case you are new to the project:

  • Site: Former Thomson View Condominium, Bright Hill Drive (District 20)
  • En bloc value: S$810 million — the largest residential en bloc approved since Chuan Park in 2023
  • Land cost: ~$1,178 psf per plot ratio (including land betterment and lease top-up)
  • Site area: ~504,314 sq ft (about 5 hectares)
  • Units: ~1,240
  • Developers: UOL Group, Singapore Land, CapitaLand Development
  • Tenure: Fresh 99-year leasehold
  • TOP target: Around 2030
  • Preview: Q3/Q4 2026
  • MRT access: Upper Thomson (TEL), Bright Hill (TEL — future Cross Island Line interchange), Marymount (Circle Line)
  • Schools: Within 1km of Ai Tong Primary School; near Catholic High and CHIJ St. Nicholas
  • Beside: MacRitchie Reservoir

Three SGX-listed developers, three MRT lines, an SAP primary school inside the 1km zone, a fresh 99-year lease, 5 hectares of land, 1,240 units. On the fundamentals checklist, this is one of the strongest launch profiles Singapore has seen this decade.

The question we are really asking is not whether Thomson Reserve is a good project. It clearly is. The question is whether it is a good buy for you, at this price. That is a different conversation. Let us have it properly.

The Five Worries Every Smart Buyer Has — And What the Data Actually Says

If you have been reading other reviews of Thomson Reserve, you will have come across the same five concerns. They are reasonable. They deserve a real answer, not a glossy deflection. Here they are, one by one.

Worry #1: "The launch price is just too high"

The concern in plain English: At $2,500–$3,100 psf, a 700 sq ft 2-bedder works out to roughly $1.75M–$2.17M. A 950 sq ft 3-bedder lands around $2.4M–$2.95M. That is a lot of money — especially for a development that has not even broken ground.

What the numbers actually say. Let's look at where that price comes from.

The S$810 million en bloc translates to a land cost of about $1,178 psf ppr. Layer on construction (~$550–$650 psf), financing and marketing (~$250–$350 psf), GST, and a 20–25% developer margin, and breakeven sits around $2,200–$2,400 psf. The launch range that emerges sits about 15–25% above breakeven — which is normal, not aggressive, for a mega launch in a prime catchment.

Now compare that to Chuan Park (the most direct recent comparable), where land cost was $1,256 psf ppr — higher — and the project launched at an average of $2,579 psf.

In other words, Thomson Reserve is being priced on the same math everyone else is being priced on. It is not a "premium grab." It is what Singapore land costs in 2026.

The honest reframe. The right question is not "Why is Thomson Reserve so expensive?" The honest question is, "Given that all new launches in this corridor will be in this band, where do I want to be inside the band?" And on that question, Thomson Reserve has a stronger argument than most.

Worry #2: "Appreciation will be too slow"

The concern in plain English: If I am paying near the top of the market, will my money even grow? Could it sit still for years?

What comparable projects actually show.

The most direct comparable to Thomson Reserve is not in Lentor and not in Chuan Park — it is right next door, on Shunfu Road. Jadescape sits in the same District 20 corridor, was redeveloped from a similar en-bloc site, is almost identical in size, and shares the same three-MRT-line connectivity profile. If any project in Singapore tells us how Thomson Reserve might behave, it is this one.

  • Jadescape (D20, 1,206 units, by Qingjian Realty). Launched in September 2018 at an indicative $1,526 PSF (after early bird discounts). Today, the development transacts at an average of S$2,322 psf, with a range of S$1,856 to S$2,620. That is roughly 52% appreciation over about 7.5 years, or 5.5–6% compounded annually — and the price kept climbing post-TOP, not just before it. The same 1,200-unit "supply will flood the market" worry was raised at Jadescape's TOP in 2022. It did not happen. Owners who held are now sitting on substantial gains, and the project continues to set new psf highs in the resale market.
  • Lentor Modern (D26, 605 units). Launched September 2022 at an average S$2,107 PSF. By TOP in August 2025, resale units were trading at S$2,239–S$2,755 PSF — a gain of 6–31% depending on unit type and floor. Median experience: roughly 15–20% over three years.
  • Lentor Hills Residences (D26, 598 units). Launched July 2023 and now trades at $2,272–$2,686 PSF — approximately 20–24% appreciation in under three years.
  • Lentor Mansion (D26, 533 units). Launched March 2024 at an average PSF of $2,104, with current transacted PSF ranging from $2,429 to $2,941 — strong early traction by the same developer profile as Thomson Reserve.
  • AMO Residence (D20, 372 units). Currently transacts at S$2,211–S$2,980 PSF, up from a 2022 launch range in the low $2,100s. Solid appreciation in the same district.
  • Thomson Three (D20, 445 units). Launched in 2013 at around S$1,325 to S$1,350 psf, now averaging S$2,094 psf — roughly 55% over 12 years, or about 4% compounded per year. The earlier benchmark on the same Bright Hill Drive stretch.

The honest pattern across this set is 5–8% compounded a year for early entrants in well-located mega launches, with the strongest performers — the first-mover, MRT-integrated, family-format projects — clustering in the upper half of the range. Jadescape, the most directly comparable to Thomson Reserve, lands cleanly in this band.

The honest reframe. "Slow" is relative. A 5–7% compounded return on a primary residence is not slow — it is the rate at which most Singaporeans actually build their first million. The expectation of 30% in three years is the speculative bull case from 2020–2022, when ABSD was lower and supply was thinner. That market is not coming back. What is left is the steady-compound market — and on those terms, Thomson Reserve is structurally better positioned than Jadescape was at launch on three measurable counts:

  1. Three MRT lines within walking distance vs Jadescape's two functional lines (Circle Line and TEL). The CRL Phase 1 interchange status at Bright Hill is a permanent uplift Jadescape does not have.
  2. A SAP primary school inside the 1km zone (Ai Tong). Jadescape's nearest priority-zone primary school catchment is materially weaker.
  3. Three SGX-listed blue-chip developers (UOL, SingLand, CapitaLand) versus Jadescape's single Chinese developer at launch. Construction quality risk and resale brand pull are both stronger.

In other words: the project that most closely resembles Thomson Reserve in size, district, and structure delivered ~5.5–6% compounded for its early buyers. Thomson Reserve enters with better fundamentals on the three variables that matter most for durable appreciation. The base case is not "slow." The base case is the rate at which Singapore property has historically built quiet, real wealth.

The Thomson Reserve buyer who is unhappy in 2030 is the buyer who expected a flip. The Thomson Reserve buyer who is content is the buyer who treated it as a long compound — exactly as Jadescape's patient owners are now.

Worry #3: "1,240 units is too much supply at TOP"

The concern in plain English: When the project completes, hundreds of owners may try to sell or rent at the same time. Won't that flood the market and depress prices?

What the data on mega projects actually shows. It is true that mega launches concentrate supply at TOP. It is also true that mega launches concentrate attention — and attention is what creates liquidity.

Look at how the market has actually behaved:

  • Jadescape is the cleanest case study. It TOP'd in 2022/2023 with 1,206 units — almost identical to Thomson Reserve's 1,240. Post-TOP, the project has averaged ~9.5% PSF appreciation between 2023 and 2026. The "supply flood" never materialised in the way pessimists predicted.
  • Chuan Park sold 696 of 916 units at an average of $2,579 psf over a single launch weekend in November 2024. By 2026, around 92% had sold, with average pricing inching up to about $2,596 psf. Concentrated supply did not collapse the price.
  • Lentor Modern, a 605-unit project, achieved 100% sales by January 2025 and is now the resale benchmark for the entire Lentor precinct.
  • Lentor Mansion sold 75% of units on launch weekend in March 2024 at an average PSF of $2,104, with current resale PSF at $2,429 to $2,941.

Mega projects in good locations consistently price-discover upward after TOP, not downward, because:

  1. Recognition effect. Buyers from across Singapore know the project by name. That brand recognition pulls in a deeper buyer pool than boutique 200-unit developments ever achieve.
  2. Liquidity for sellers. When you do want to sell, there are buyers actively comparing your unit to listings inside the same project. Boutique projects suffer from "no recent comparable" pricing dead zones; mega projects do not.
  3. Rental absorption. Large unit counts also mean large rental inventory — but in Singapore's expat and domestic rental market, this is matched by deeper rental demand to exactly the projects renters search for.

The honest reframe. Supply at TOP is real, but it is also temporary — typically 6–18 months of elevated subsale activity, after which the project settles into its long-term resale rhythm. Owners who hold past that initial window historically transact at higher prices than the early-TOP subsale wave. Translation: if you can hold past 2032, the supply argument largely dissolves.

Worry #4: "Isn't the CRL already priced in?"

The concern in plain English: Cross Island Line Phase 1 (around 2030) is the big future catalyst. But surely the developers already know that — and that is exactly why they bid S$810 million for the site. So is the upside already gone?

What the analysis actually shows. This is a genuinely smart question, and the answer is nuanced. Yes, the developers priced some CRL premium into their land bid. No, they did not price all of it — because they cannot.

Here is why. When LTA confirms an MRT line years in advance, the announcement premium gets capitalised into nearby property within months. From 2021 to mid-2025, the average resale price for 99-year leasehold condos in District 20 rose by 31.6% to $1,905 psf. Much of that already happened.

What is not yet capitalised is the operational premium — the further uplift that occurs once the line actually opens, commuter behaviour shifts, and integration with the existing TEL and Circle Line networks becomes lived experience. Historically, MRT-adjacent properties in Singapore see a second wave of appreciation in the 12–24 months after a station opens, on top of the announcement premium they have already received.

Bright Hill MRT is set to become an interchange between TEL and CRL Phase 1. That kind of interchange status — three MRT lines within walking distance — is held by fewer than 15 condo projects in Singapore. It is a permanent structural advantage that compounds over the entire 99-year lease.

The honest reframe. The CRL is partially priced in. The interchange uplift is not. The 99-year residual value of being a three-line condo is certainly not. You are paying for the obvious. You are not paying for the durable.

Worry #5: "What if Thomson Reserve becomes another Thomson Grand?"

The concern in plain English: Thomson Grand, in the same broader area, famously underperformed — only a 9% price appreciation with an average profit of about $146,000. What stops Thomson Reserve from going the same way?

What was actually different about Thomson Grand. This is one of the most quoted cautionary tales in Singapore property — but the reasons it underperformed are specific, and they do not transfer to Thomson Reserve.

Thomson Grand suffered from:

  • Layout inefficiency. A private lift lobby on multiple units consumed strata area that buyers were paying for but could not use, hurting effective PSF perception on resale.
  • MRT distance. It launched before the TEL existed in the area — its location was not yet supported by direct MRT access.
  • Smaller scale and weaker brand pull. Without the recognition effect of a mega launch, its resale liquidity stayed thin for years.

Thomson Reserve has the opposite of those problems on every count: a 1,240-unit mega scale, three MRT lines within walking distance, three blue-chip developer brands, and an open-floor design philosophy the consortium has refined across Parktown, Watten House, and other recent projects.

The honest reframe. Thomson Grand is a cautionary tale about unit design and connectivity, not about location. The Thomson postcode itself has performed perfectly well — Thomson Three, in the same Bright Hill Drive stretch, delivered a 20% price appreciation and an average profit of around $251,000. The lesson is to buy efficient layouts in well-connected mega projects. That is precisely what Thomson Reserve is designed to be.

The Three Quiet Edges Most Reviews Skip

If you have read this far, you are exactly the kind of buyer who deserves the parts of the analysis that brochures and quick reviews leave out.

1. The Ai Tong 1km Zone Is Genuinely Rare

Within Singapore's most competitive primary school catchment system, the 1km radius around an SAP (Special Assistance Plan) primary school is one of the few structural advantages that does not depreciate with lease. Ai Tong Primary is one of the most subscribed primary schools in Singapore, and the 1km zone around Bright Hill Drive places Thomson Reserve residents in Phase 2C(S) priority — which, in oversubscribed years, is the deciding factor.

For families with children under seven, this is not a soft amenity. It is a hard-edged practical reason that supports both purchase demand and rental demand for the next 30 years, regardless of what the broader market is doing. Family demand is the most price-resilient demand in Singapore real estate.

2. Three Blue-Chip Developers Behave Differently

When a single developer launches a project, they price aggressively early to clear inventory. When three SGX-listed developers — each with their own boards, balance sheets, and reputation considerations — co-launch, the pricing tends to be steadier and the construction quality more consistent. Reputational risk is shared, which makes shortcuts costly to all parties.

The same consortium (UOL, SingLand, CapitaLand) was the one behind Parktown Residence, which sold 87% of its units on launch weekend in February 2025. The combined balance sheet and the track record matter when you are paying $2M+ for an unbuilt unit. Default risk and construction-defect risk are not zero in Singapore — but they are about as close to zero as any private project gets.

3. The 99-Year Clock Is Reset

This sounds technical, but it matters. The original Thomson View was leasehold from 1975. The redevelopment includes a lease top-up to a fresh 99 years. For a buyer with a 15–25 year hold horizon, the lease decay impact is much smaller than buying a 15-year-old project in the same area today.

Lease decay accelerates after the 30-year mark. A fresh-99 project gives you a long runway where lease decay is a negligible drag on resale. Compare to a 2010-vintage condo in the same area, which is already 16 years into its lease and starts seeing meaningful lease-related discounting from its 30th year onward.

Who Thomson Reserve Genuinely Fits — And Who Should Reconsider

This is the part most reviews dodge. A clear answer is more useful than a "depends" non-answer.

Thomson Reserve fits you well if you are:

  • A Singapore Citizen first-property buyer (0% ABSD) planning a 7–15 year hold. The math here is very kind: zero ABSD, no rush to sell, full compounding of a structural growth corridor.
  • A family upgrader with children under seven targeting Ai Tong School or one of the other Bishan-Thomson primary schools. The school catchment is rarely fully priced into a unit's PSF.
  • A HDB upgrader from Bishan, Toa Payoh, Ang Mo Kio, or Serangoon who has built up equity in their flat and wants to lock in a fresh-99 large-format development before the next round of supply arrives. You are not paying tomorrow's price — you are getting in before the broader corridor reprices in 2027–2028.
  • A long-term investor willing to hold past TOP and through at least one full SSD cycle. Three MRT lines, two prestige primary schools, and a 5-hectare site is the kind of foundation that compounds quietly for a decade.

Thomson Reserve may not fit you well if you are:

  • A pure 5-year flipper counting on 30%+ gains. The Singapore market that delivered those returns no longer exists. This is not a Thomson Reserve issue — it is a Singapore-in-2026 issue, and the same caution applies to every other current launch.
  • A second-property buyer paying 20% ABSD who has not stress-tested the math on a 10-year hold. ABSD compresses your effective return — but only on short horizons. On a 10–15 year horizon, even a 20% ABSD second-property buyer can reach a healthy compounded return.
  • A foreign buyer (60% ABSD) without a multi-generational hold horizon.

If you are in the first list, Thomson Reserve is a genuine and rare opportunity to enter a corridor with structural tailwinds at the first wave of pricing, before the surrounding GLS launches reset the benchmark upward. If you are in the second list, there is no shame in waiting — but recognise that waiting in cash also has a cost, and the next launches in the same corridor will not be cheaper.

How to Enter Thomson Reserve Intelligently (If You Decide To)

If your honest analysis brings you to "yes" — or even a careful "yes, with conditions" — here is how disciplined buyers approach a launch like this.

  1. Pick layout efficiency over unit type. The 3-bedroom with yard, household shelter, and proper utility WC has historically outperformed compact "dumbbell" 3-bedders on resale. Bigger is not always better; more functional is.
  2. Avoid the floor right above a premium jump. Developers often layer in larger premium jumps at the 8th, 13th, or 18th floor mark. Buying the cheapest unit on the higher tier often beats buying the most expensive unit on the lower tier.
  3. Model your real all-in cost on iras.gov.sg. Don't estimate BSD and ABSD. Compute them. Then add legal, mortgage interest, maintenance, and property tax over your hold horizon.
  4. Have a clear hold horizon before you visit the showflat. Decide your minimum hold before you fall in love with a view. The 4-year SSD window plus a 2–3 year post-SSD settling period means the realistic minimum hold for net-positive returns is 6–7 years.
  5. Build in a stress-test buffer. If you can still service the mortgage at a 4.5% interest rate and one income gap of three months, you are properly hedged. If not, downsize the unit before you commit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Thomson Reserve overpriced? +

Not by 2026 standards. At an indicative S$2,500–S$3,100 psf, Thomson Reserve is priced in line with comparable Singapore RCR mega launches and slightly below Chuan Park's land-cost-implied range. The pricing is expensive, but not out of line with the market. For long-horizon owner-occupiers and Singapore Citizen first-property buyers, the entry price is well-supported by location, schools, and three-line MRT access.

Why is Thomson Reserve so expensive? +

Because the land cost is. The S$810 million en bloc translates to a land cost of about S$1,178 psf per plot ratio. Construction (~S$550–S$650 psf), financing, marketing, GST, and a typical 20–25% developer margin push launch pricing to the S$2,500–S$3,100 psf range. The price reflects what Singapore land costs in 2026, not aggressive developer positioning.

Thomson Reserve has structural advantages Chuan Park does not: three MRT lines (Chuan Park has one), the Ai Tong 1km school zone, and proximity to MacRitchie Reservoir. Same league, different strengths.

Will Thomson Reserve appreciate slowly? +

Probably steadily, not slowly. Comparable mega launches — Jadescape, Lentor Modern, Lentor Mansion, Chuan Park — have delivered 5–8% compounded annual returns for early entrants. That is the historical "base rate" for well-located Singapore mega projects, not a worst case.

What is the realistic 5-year return on Thomson Reserve? +

Base case: 15–25% over five years, gross. For a Singapore Citizen first-property buyer (0% ABSD), this translates to a healthy 12–20% net return after stamp duty, mortgage interest, and maintenance. For a second-property buyer paying 20% ABSD, five years is too short — the maths only works comfortably on a 7–10 year hold.

Is District 20 a good investment in 2026? +

Yes, structurally — though not cheaply. From 2021 to mid-2025, the average resale price for 99-year leasehold condos in District 20 rose by 31.6% to $1,905 psf, outpacing the previous five-year stretch. Three MRT lines, strong primary school network, mature retail at Thomson Plaza, and proximity to MacRitchie all support a defensible long-term thesis.

Should I wait for the next launch instead? +

Wait if the next launch is in the same corridor; act if it is not. No upcoming launch in 2026–2027 matches Thomson Reserve's combination of three-line MRT access, Ai Tong 1km zone, and 5-hectare site in the Bishan-Thomson belt. Waiting in cash also has a cost.

The Honest Bottom Line

Thomson Reserve is expensive. We should not pretend otherwise. Anyone who tells you it is a bargain is selling you a story.

But "expensive" is not the same as "wrong." Singapore land in 2026 is expensive. All the alternatives in this corridor are expensive. The relevant question is not whether the price is high — the relevant question is whether the entry point unlocks something the cheaper alternatives cannot.

On the evidence: three MRT lines, an SAP primary school in the 1km zone, a fresh 99-year lease, three blue-chip developers, 5 hectares of land beside a reservoir, and one of the rarest combinations of family-residential and capital-residential fundamentals Singapore has launched this decade — yes, the entry point unlocks something real.

The most directly comparable project in Singapore — Jadescape, on the same Bishan-Thomson corridor — has rewarded its patient owners with roughly 5.5–6% compounded annual returns since 2018. Thomson Reserve enters with stronger fundamentals on the three variables that actually drive long-term appreciation. The historical pattern does not guarantee the future, but it does tell you what kind of project this is, and what kind of holding horizon it rewards.

That does not make it right for everyone. A 5-year flipper, a stretched second-property buyer, a foreigner without a long horizon — these buyers should look elsewhere, and there is no shame in it.

But for the Singaporean first-property buyer, the family upgrader, the patient HDB-to-condo upgrader from the surrounding estates — Thomson Reserve is exactly the kind of disciplined long-compound entry that has historically built quiet, durable wealth in this country. Not flashy. Not Instagram-worthy. Just structurally sound.

The buyers who regret their property decisions in Singapore are rarely the ones who paid a fair price for a strong corridor and held patiently. They are the ones who chased a cheaper price in a weaker location, or who tried to flip in three years what a market needs ten years to deliver.

Make sure your decision is the patient one. Whatever you decide.
