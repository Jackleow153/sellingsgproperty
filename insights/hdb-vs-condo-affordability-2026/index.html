Can I Afford a $1.7M Condo If I Can Afford a $970k HDB? A Singapore Affordability Reality Check

The Short Answer

Yes — in most cases, if you can afford the HDB, you can afford the condo too.

Under the Monetary Authority of Singapore's stress-test rules (4.0% floor rate), a $1,700,000 private condo requires a minimum gross household income of $11,067 per month to qualify. A $970,000 HDB resale flat requires $12,800 per month. The condo needs $1,733 LESS in monthly income because it falls under TDSR (55% cap) instead of MSR (30% cap), and permits a 30-year loan tenure versus 25 years for HDB resale bank loans at maximum LTV. Whether you should stretch to the condo is a separate question — this article walks through both sides.

It's the most common misconception I hear at first buyer consultations: "I can only afford the HDB, so the condo is off the table." When we run the numbers using the Monetary Authority of Singapore's actual affordability rules, the picture almost always looks different. This isn't a spin or a sales trick — it's a direct consequence of how MAS structures debt-servicing limits and loan tenures differently for public and private housing.

This article walks through the full comparison between a $970,000 HDB resale flat and a $1,700,000 private resale condominium in Singapore, using 2026 rates and rules. I'll show you the affordability math, the upfront cash and CPF requirements, why the counter-intuitive result happens, and — importantly — the trade-offs you must accept if you decide to stretch. Every regulatory figure in this piece is cited against MAS, IRAS, HDB, or CPF sources at the end.

How affordability is actually assessed in Singapore

Before comparing the two properties, it helps to understand what a bank actually checks when deciding how much you can borrow. There are three levers that determine your maximum loan quantum in Singapore:

  1. Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratio — how much of the property price the bank will finance. For a first housing loan with a tenure of 30 years or less (and where the loan ends before you turn 65), the cap is 75%. You put down the remaining 25%.
  2. Debt-servicing ratio — how much of your gross income can go toward loan repayment. For HDB flats and Executive Condominiums, this is the Mortgage Servicing Ratio (MSR) at 30%. For private property, it's the Total Debt Servicing Ratio (TDSR) at 55%.
  3. Stress-test interest rate — the "medium-term interest rate floor" MAS requires banks to use when sizing your loan. Since 30 September 2022, this has been 4.0% per annum for bank loans and 3.0% for HDB concessionary loans, regardless of the actual promo rate offered.
Quick Answer

Why does MAS stress-test at 4%? Property loans are long-term commitments and typically households' largest liability. MAS requires banks to size your maximum loan as if you were paying 4% p.a., even if your actual rate is currently 1.4%. This ensures you can still service the loan if rates rise significantly over the next 25 to 30 years.

Both frameworks apply the same 4% stress floor. So on the surface, you might expect the more expensive property to require more income. Let's see what actually happens.

Why does the condo need less qualifying income than the HDB?

The result surprises most first-time buyers because it inverts the intuition that "more expensive equals harder to afford." Two regulatory levers produce the crossover:

Lever 1: TDSR 55% is more permissive than MSR 30%

Mortgage Servicing Ratio (MSR) restricts your monthly housing loan repayment to 30% of gross monthly income and applies only to HDB flats and Executive Condominiums. Total Debt Servicing Ratio (TDSR) caps total monthly debt at 55% and applies to all property loans, public and private.

Even though TDSR counts all your debt obligations (car loan, credit card minimums, personal loan repayments), if a buyer has no other significant debt, they effectively get to use up to 55% of gross income for the mortgage. That's 25 percentage points more headroom than MSR gives.

Quick Answer

Does the same buyer qualify under both frameworks? Yes, but the maximum loan sizes differ dramatically. On $12,800/month gross income, MSR allows $3,840/month of mortgage servicing while TDSR would allow up to $7,040/month (assuming no other debt). That's why private property affordability scales up much faster with income.

Lever 2: 30-year tenure vs 25-year tenure

Under MAS rules, HDB resale bank loans are capped at 25 years at full 75% LTV, while private property bank loans allow up to 30 years. Longer tenures are technically possible (up to 30 years for HDB and 35 years for private property) but LTV drops from 75% to 55% — requiring a much larger downpayment.

Those five extra years matter. Spreading the same principal amount over 60 more monthly instalments materially reduces the stress-tested monthly payment used in the income test. Combined with the more generous debt cap, the effect more than compensates for the larger loan quantum.

The condo requires roughly $235,000 more in total outlay. But the composition of that outlay matters more than the headline number:

  • $340,000 of the condo downpayment is CPF-eligible. For most working buyers who've contributed to CPF for a decade or more, this is money that's already earmarked for housing under CPF rules — parked in the Ordinary Account earning 2.5% p.a. Redeploying it into a private asset in your name is not "more cash out" so much as "more CPF put to work."
  • The HDB scenario shown includes $70,000 in Cash Above Valuation. When the transacted price exceeds HDB's valuation (here, $970k vs $900k), the shortfall must be paid in cash — it cannot use CPF or loan. This is a common feature of the current resale HDB market and eats into the perceived cash advantage of HDB.
  • ABSD 5% for a PR 1st property is $85,000 on the $1.7M condo — a real cost. If the buyer were a Singapore Citizen (0% ABSD on first property), that difference disappears entirely.

The long view: why the higher monthly payment is not just an expense

At today's rates, the condo commits you to roughly $1,400 more per month in mortgage repayment than the HDB. That number needs unpacking, because a meaningful portion of every repayment is principal — equity built in a hard asset in your own name, not interest expense paid to the bank.

The forced-savings argument, honestly framed

Think of the extra monthly commitment as a disciplined savings vehicle parked in Singapore real estate. It is non-negotiable (unlike voluntary savings), hedged against long-term inflation, and secured by an asset that has historically appreciated over full market cycles.

The trade-off is real: higher monthly commitment demands financial discipline and a healthy cash buffer. But over a 20- to 30-year horizon, the outcome is a materially larger net-asset position — provided the buyer stays employed and the property performs in line with the private market's long-run trend.

What the long-term data actually shows

Both the URA Private Residential Property Price Index and the HDB Resale Price Index are rebased to 1Q 2009 = 100. As at 4Q 2025 (the latest full quarter data from SingStat):

  • URA Private Residential Price Index: 216.4 — up 116% from 2009
  • HDB Resale Price Index: 203.6 — up 104% from 2009

Over 17 years, private residential has outperformed HDB resale by roughly 13 index points, or around 12 percentage points cumulative outperformance. In Q1 2026, HDB resale prices posted their first quarterly decline in nearly seven years while private prices continued to rise. Structural factors matter: HDB flats face MOP restrictions, ethnic quotas at resale, 99-year lease decay, and policy interventions when prices climb too fast. Private residential has none of these caps.

None of this is a guarantee of future returns. But it does inform a straightforward analytical view: if wealth accumulation is a real objective alongside own-stay, the private market gives you a vehicle designed to appreciate. Public housing is designed to remain affordable, which is a fundamentally different mandate.

The trade-offs you must accept if you stretch to the condo

The math above is real. It is also incomplete. Here are the concrete downsides of moving from a $970k HDB to a $1.7M private condo:

Cash flow and liquidity

  • Actual monthly repayment is ~$1,400 higher at today's rates — and rates can move up. If SORA rises 100 basis points, that gap widens by roughly $600/month.
  • Condo maintenance fees typically run $300 to $600 per month depending on the estate — the HDB service and conservancy charge is a fraction of this.
  • Property tax scales with annual value; on a $1.7M condo occupied by owner, property tax is materially higher than on a $970k HDB.

Upfront capital

  • Mandatory 5% cash of $85,000 vs $45,000 for HDB.
  • ABSD 5% adds $85,000 for a PR — this money is a pure sunk cost.
  • Higher BSD adds $30,900 relative to the HDB.

Market risk

  • Private property prices have historically appreciated more but with higher volatility. The 2018 cooling measures and 2023 ABSD hike both produced short-term market softness.
  • Historical outperformance is not a guarantee. If you must sell during a soft cycle, the equity gain evaporates.
Important caveat for PR-only households. The HDB concessionary loan at 2.6% p.a. is not available to households without at least one Singapore Citizen. A Singapore PR-only couple buying an HDB resale flat must take a bank loan — which means both scenarios in this article are being compared like-for-like on a bank loan basis. If at least one buyer is a Singapore Citizen, the HDB concessionary loan option would apply to the HDB (not the condo), which offers rate stability at 2.6% but is currently more expensive than bank rates.

Who should seriously consider the condo instead of the HDB?

Based on the numbers, the stretch to a private condo is worth serious consideration if the following apply to your household:

  1. Gross household income comfortably above $11,000/month — ideally $13,000 to $15,000 or higher, so the actual repayment sits below 30% of income and there's cash-flow headroom.
  2. Sufficient CPF-OA balances to fund most or all of the 20% downpayment portion — ideally at least $250,000 to $340,000 in combined household CPF-OA.
  3. At least $170,000 to $200,000 in cash reserves to cover the 5% cash downpayment, stamp duties, and a 12-month emergency buffer.
  4. A minimum 7- to 10-year holding horizon. Private property rewards long holds; short flips are punished by SSD and ABSD structures.
  5. Job stability or dual-income households that can absorb the higher monthly commitment without stress.
  6. Own-stay plus wealth accumulation is a real goal — not just shelter.

Conversely, the HDB remains the right answer if you value flat monthly costs, want to maximise cash liquidity, or prefer the concessionary loan's 2.6% rate stability (if eligible). There is no wrong answer — only a fit answer for your household's circumstances.

Frequently asked questions

Can I afford a $1.7M condo if I can afford a $970k HDB in Singapore?

Yes, in most cases. Under MAS stress-test rules (4.0% floor rate), a $1.7M private condo requires a minimum gross household income of $11,067 per month to qualify, while a $970k HDB requires $12,800 per month. The condo needs $1,733 less because it uses TDSR (55% cap) instead of MSR (30% cap), and allows a 30-year loan tenure versus 25 years for HDB resale bank loans at 75% LTV. However, actual cash outlay is higher for the condo, so affordability must be assessed on both dimensions.

What is the difference between MSR and TDSR in Singapore?

Mortgage Servicing Ratio (MSR) caps your monthly housing loan repayment at 30% of gross monthly income and applies only to HDB flats and Executive Condominiums. Total Debt Servicing Ratio (TDSR) caps total monthly debt at 55% of gross monthly income and applies to all property loans, including private residential. TDSR includes all debts (car, credit card, personal loan) while MSR looks only at the housing mortgage. Both are enforced by MAS.

What is the maximum loan tenure for HDB and private property in Singapore?

For 75% loan-to-value, HDB resale bank loans are capped at 25 years and private residential bank loans at 30 years. HDB concessionary loans are also capped at 25 years. Longer tenures are possible (up to 30 years for HDB and 35 years for private) but LTV drops to 55%, requiring a much larger downpayment. In all cases, the loan must end before the borrower turns 65 to qualify for the maximum LTV.

How much ABSD does a Singapore PR pay on a first property in 2026?

A Singapore Permanent Resident pays 5% Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty (ABSD) on their first residential property, calculated on the higher of purchase price or market value. Singapore Citizens pay 0% on their first property, and foreigners pay 60%. Rates are set by IRAS and have been unchanged since 27 April 2023. On a $1.7M condo, PR ABSD is $85,000.

What is the Buyer's Stamp Duty on a $1.7 million property in Singapore?

The Buyer's Stamp Duty (BSD) on a $1,700,000 residential property is $54,600. This uses IRAS's progressive tiers: 1% on the first $180,000 ($1,800), 2% on the next $180,000 ($3,600), 3% on the next $640,000 ($19,200), 4% on the next $500,000 ($20,000), and 5% on the remaining $200,000 ($10,000). BSD is payable within 14 days of signing the Sale and Purchase Agreement.

Can I use CPF to pay for a private condo downpayment in Singapore?

Yes. For a private residential purchase with a 25% downpayment, the mandatory 5% must be paid in cash, but the remaining 20% can be paid using CPF Ordinary Account (OA) savings, cash, or a combination of both. On a $1.7 million condo, that means up to $340,000 of the downpayment can be funded from CPF-OA. CPF can also be used to pay BSD, ABSD, monthly instalments and legal fees.

Does the HDB concessionary loan at 2.6% apply to Singapore PRs?

No. The HDB concessionary loan requires at least one Singapore Citizen in the household. A Singapore PR-only couple purchasing an HDB resale flat must take a bank loan, which currently offers rates from approximately 1.4% to 1.7% p.a. as of mid-2026. The concessionary rate is pegged at 0.1% above the CPF Ordinary Account rate (currently 2.5%), giving 2.6% p.a.

Is a condo really a better investment than an HDB in Singapore?

Historically, yes — but modestly. From 1Q 2009 to 4Q 2025, the URA Private Residential Property Price Index rose 116% while the HDB Resale Price Index rose 104%. Private property also offers no MOP, no ethnic quota, no income ceiling, and a larger resale buyer pool (including foreigners and PRs), giving it more upside optionality. However, private is more volatile, has higher carrying costs, and historical returns do not guarantee future returns.
