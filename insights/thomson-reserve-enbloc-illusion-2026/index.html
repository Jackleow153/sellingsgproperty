Thomson Reserve and the $810 Million Illusion: Why a Big En Bloc Price Is Not Proof You Should Buy

Quick Answer

The $810 million paid for the former Thomson View site does not mean Thomson Reserve is a good buy for you. It means the land was expensive for the developers. That is their cost, and they will pass it on to you. A big, confident number from three famous developers is one of the most powerful psychological triggers in property marketing, and most buyers never notice it working on them. This article is about that trigger, why it fools smart people, and how to switch it off before you sign anything.

This is not a reason to avoid Thomson Reserve. It is a reason to buy it (or not) for the right reasons instead of a borrowed one.

The number that is doing your thinking for you

Let's start with the headline everyone repeats: three of Singapore's biggest developers — UOL, Singapore Land and CapitaLand — paid $810 million for the former Thomson View site. It was the largest approved en bloc since Chuan Park ($890 million) in 2023.

Now watch what that number does in your head. It whispers:

"If the smartest, richest developers in Singapore paid this much, the location must be worth it. They know something. I can trust this."

That feeling is real. It is also a trap with a name. Behavioural economists call it anchoring — the mind grabs the first big number it sees and quietly measures everything else against it. Beside it sits authority bias — we assume experts (here, big developers) must be right, so we borrow their confidence instead of doing our own.

Put the two together and you get the most effective sales tool at any new launch. Nobody has to say a word. The $810 million says it for them.

Here is the uncomfortable truth: a high land price tells you what the developer spent, not what you should pay. Those are two completely different things, and the whole game is getting you to confuse them.

"Smart money bought here" — the fallacy in one line

You will hear a version of this everywhere: smart money moved in, so you should follow.

It sounds obvious. It is also wrong, for a simple reason most buyers never think through.

Developers and you are not making the same trade.

The developer's trade vs. yours
 The developerYou
What they buyLand, in bulkOne finished unit
How they profitSelling ~1,268 units at a marginSelling one unit, years later
Their timelineSell out in a few yearsHold 5–15 years
Their safety netBillion-dollar balance sheetYour single household income
A soft market meansSlightly thinner marginReal money, out of your pocket

The developer wins on volume and margin, spread across more than a thousand units, cushioned by a balance sheet that can absorb a bad year without blinking. You win only if your one unit sells for more than you paid, after stamp duties, interest and fees, on your own timeline, with your own mortgage.

So when someone says "the developers clearly believe in this location," the honest reply is: of course they do — they've priced in a profit on 1,268 sales. You've priced in nothing yet. Their confidence is not transferable to you. It was never designed to be.

What the "winners" of this en bloc actually reveal

Here is a detail the marketing never dwells on. The Thomson View owners originally wanted $918 million. They eventually accepted $810 million — about 12% less than their asking price, after a seventeen-year, five-attempt en bloc saga.

Read that again. Even the people celebrated as the big winners of this deal compromised. They held out for nearly two decades and still took less than they hoped for.

That is not a criticism of them. It is a quiet lesson about this exact plot: getting full value out of it has never been easy, even for the sellers who owned it outright with no mortgage and no rush.

And now you are being invited onto the other side of that same trade — buying the replacement units at a fresh, full, 2026 price. The sellers realised their money and walked away. Your gain is still a bet. Those are not the same position, no matter how the story is framed.

The anchor that history quietly disproves

Let's test the "high land price = good buy" idea against real transactions on this very corridor. I'll use three completed condos with actual track records: JadeScape, Thomson Three and Thomson Grand. Notice what happened to the people who bought at the lowest anchors.

  • JadeScape's big winners bought in around $1,700 psf in 2018. One penthouse bought at $5.8 million in 2019 sold for $10.15 million in 2024 — a $4.35 million gain. The profit was born the day they bought cheap, not the day they sold.
  • Thomson Three buyers entered on a 2012 land price that the market laughed at at the time — there was no MRT yet. 99% of resales later turned a profit (average around $251,720). They were rewarded for buying before the anchor rose.
  • Thomson Grand, meanwhile, is the cautionary tale. Ten years old, same district, next to an exclusive country club — and still the weakest of the three at ~$1,809 psf. Some early buyers even resold at a loss (one four-bedroom took a ~$60,000 hit in 2018). A "prestige" location did not save them.

See the pattern? Every big win on this corridor came from buying at a low anchor before the crowd caught on. The $1,178 psf ppr behind Thomson Reserve is the highest anchor this stretch has ever seen. You are not being offered the JadeScape-2018 entry. You are being offered the top of the ladder built on all those earlier, cheaper rungs.

That does not make it a bad home. It makes it a very different bet from the ones people quote to sell it to you.

Why Singaporeans are especially wired for this

We need to be honest about ourselves here, because the anchor works better on us than on almost anyone.

Property in Singapore is not just an asset. It is identity, security, and proof you made it. It is the thing your relatives ask about at reunion dinner. It is the safe place to put money in a country where property has, for a generation, mostly gone up.

That emotional weight makes us vulnerable in three specific ways:

  1. We fear missing out more than we fear overpaying. The pain of watching prices rise after we hesitated feels worse than the slow, quiet pain of paying too much. So a big, urgent number pushes us toward "just buy it."
  2. We treat developer confidence as permission. "If UOL and CapitaLand believe in it, who am I to doubt it?" It feels humble. It is actually just outsourcing your biggest financial decision to someone whose interests are not yours.
  3. We anchor on the headline, not the household. We remember "$810 million" and "$1,178 psf." We forget to run our own sums — the loan, the five years of payments with no rent, the stamp duty.

None of this makes you foolish. It makes you human, and Singaporean, and exactly the buyer this kind of launch is built for. The way out is not to feel bad about it. It is to see it, and then decide with your eyes open.

How to switch the anchor off (a 4-question reset)

Before your next showflat visit, answer these four questions honestly. If the anchor is doing your thinking, you will feel resistance to at least one of them. That resistance is the point.

  1. Would I still want this unit if the land had cost half as much? The land price is the developer's problem, not your home. If a cheaper land cost would make you less keen, you are buying the number, not the property.
  2. What is my exit price, and who is my future buyer? Be specific. In 2032, who buys your unit, and at what psf? If you can't answer, you don't have a plan — you have a hope.
  3. Can I hold this for ten years without strain if prices go flat? New launches pay in stages and earn no rent for ~5 years until TOP (around 2031 here). Model a boring market, not a booming one.
  4. Am I buying because it fits my life, or because a big number told me it's safe? Only the first reason survives a downturn. The second one evaporates the moment the market wobbles.

If you pass all four, you are not being anchored — you are making a genuine decision, and Thomson Reserve may well be right for you. If you flinch, you just saved yourself from the most expensive mistake in property: buying someone else's confidence.

So should you avoid Thomson Reserve?

No. And I want to be clear, because this piece is deliberately provocative and I don't want it misread.

Everything good about Thomson Reserve is real. Three MRT stations within walking distance. A fresh 99-year lease. Ai Tong School within 1km. A permanently protected reservoir boundary that can never be built over. Developers who will absolutely deliver. On this same street, 99% of Thomson Three resellers made money. These are not small things.

The point of this article is narrower and, I think, more useful than another "pros and cons" list:

A high en bloc price is a reason to do more homework, not less. It is the developer's cost, dressed up as your validation.

Strip the $810 million out of your head. Then look at the unit, the location, your loan, and your ten-year plan. If it still makes sense — buy it with confidence. If it only made sense because three big names paid a lot, you were never really deciding. The number was.

The best buyers in Singapore are not the ones who chase the biggest launch. They are the ones who can look at $810 million, feel the pull, and still ask, calmly: "Yes, but is it right for me?"

Be that buyer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did Thomson View sell for $810 million?

Three developers (UOL, Singapore Land, CapitaLand) bought the site to redevelop it into Thomson Reserve. At $810 million it was the largest approved collective sale since Chuan Park ($890 million) in 2023, working out to about $1,178 psf ppr after land betterment charges and a lease top-up.

Does a high en bloc price mean the new condo is a good buy?

Not by itself. A high land price is the developer's cost, which they build into your purchase price. It tells you the location is in demand, but it does not tell you whether your entry price leaves room for your profit. Those are separate questions.

Is Thomson Reserve overpriced?

No price has been released, so this can't be answered yet. What we know is that the land cost (~$1,178 psf ppr) is the highest on this corridor to date — more than double what Thomson Grand's land cost in 2009. Whether the launch price is fair depends entirely on where the developer sets it above that base. Wait for the official price list.

Did buyers at nearby condos actually make money?

Mostly yes — but the winners bought early and cheap. JadeScape's biggest gains came from ~$1,700 psf entries in 2018 (one penthouse gained $4.35 million). Thomson Three saw 99% profitable resales (average ~$251,720). Thomson Grand, bought at higher relative anchors later, was weakest and included some resale losses.

What is "anchoring" in property buying?

Anchoring is a mental shortcut where your brain fixes on the first big number it sees and judges everything else against it. In new launches, the en bloc land price or a headline psf becomes the anchor, making the eventual launch price feel "reasonable by comparison" even when it isn't. Awareness is the main defence.

Is Thomson Reserve freehold?

No. It is 99-year leasehold, topped up to a fresh 99 years. Some websites wrongly label it freehold — always confirm tenure yourself before believing a listing.

When does Thomson Reserve launch and TOP?

Launch is expected in the second half of 2026 (Q3 or October, unconfirmed). Estimated TOP is around 2031. Plan for roughly five years of progressive payments with no rental income before you can move in or rent out.

Should I buy Thomson Reserve at launch or wait?

It depends on your goal. If you want a long-term home in this exact catchment, launch gives you the freshest lease and first pick of stacks. If you are investment-led, it can be worth watching the resale and sub-sale market a few years after TOP, when some launch buyers need to exit — a pattern visible in older projects on this corridor.
